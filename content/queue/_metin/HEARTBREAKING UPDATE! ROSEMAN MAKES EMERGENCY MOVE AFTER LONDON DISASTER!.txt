According to Andrew DiCecco of Inside the Birds, the Eagles brought back defensive tackle Zion Wilson to the practice squad. Twenty-three years old. Six foot three. Around three hundred sixteen pounds. And if you have never heard that name before, do not feel bad. Until about a year ago, almost nobody had. But Eagles Nation, I need you to hear this story, because it is one of the most unlikely paths to an NFL building I have ever seen.

Zion Wilson arrived at East Carolina as a walk-on. No scholarship. No promises. No guarantee of anything. And here is the part that blows my mind. He was not even a defensive player. He came in as an offensive lineman. He spent his first two years on the scout team, the guys who line up in practice to give the starters a look, the guys whose names never show up in a box score.

Then in 2023, he played three offensive snaps. Three. And fifty-one snaps on special teams. In 2024, the coaches moved him to the defensive line. And how many defensive snaps did he play that year? Zero. Seventy snaps on special teams, zero on defense. Jimmy Kempski of PhillyVoice did the math, and across his first four college seasons, Zion Wilson played a grand total of three snaps on offense or defense. Three snaps in four years.

Think about that for a second. Think about how many young men in that situation just walk away. They transfer to a smaller school, or they hang up the cleats and get on with life. And nobody would blame them. But Zion Wilson stayed. He kept working. He kept showing up for practice, kept doing the dirty work on the scout team, kept waiting for his chance.

And then 2025 happened. Out of nowhere, and that is literally how Kempski described it, out of nowhere, Wilson became a full-time starter in the middle of the East Carolina defensive line. Thirteen games. Forty-two tackles. Ten tackles for loss. Seven sacks. Seven sacks from a guy who, one year earlier, had not played a single defensive snap. Kempski described him as having a squatty build with some quickness, and when you watch a three-hundred-sixteen-pound man get into the backfield like that, quickness is exactly the word.

Now, here is where the story takes another turn. Zion Wilson was not even planning to go to the NFL in 2026. He had committed to the University of Virginia to play one more year of college football. One more season to build his film, one more season to prove the breakout was real. But the NCAA denied his waiver for a sixth year of eligibility. Door closed.

And that is when the Philadelphia Eagles stepped in. On June fifth, the Eagles signed him as an undrafted free agent, and to make room they released wide receiver Brandon Hayes. And this was not just a throw-in camp body contract. According to Ryan Fowler of NBC Sports Philadelphia, Wilson got a twenty-five-thousand-dollar signing bonus and two hundred twenty-five thousand dollars guaranteed. Kempski laid out the numbers for the whole undrafted class, and Wilson’s guarantee was among the highest in that group. Not the very top, but right up there. And Eagles Nation, that is a message. When this front office puts real guaranteed money on an undrafted kid, it means somebody in that building saw something on tape that they liked.

He went through all of training camp with the Eagles. He learned the system. He sat in that defensive line meeting room. The reports we have do not say exactly where he was in between. What we do know is that now, in the middle of a three-game losing streak, with the defensive front banged up, Philadelphia went back and picked up the phone.

So the question is, why him, and why now?

Here is my take, and I think it is pretty simple. When you bring back a player who already knows your system, you save time. Zion Wilson already knows the terminology. He already knows the coaches. He already spent weeks inside that building learning how Vic Fangio wants his front to play. He does not need a crash course. He does not need three weeks to figure out where the cafeteria is. He just needs an opportunity. And when you are in week six and the injuries are piling up, you cannot afford to teach everything from scratch to someone who has never set foot in your facility.
