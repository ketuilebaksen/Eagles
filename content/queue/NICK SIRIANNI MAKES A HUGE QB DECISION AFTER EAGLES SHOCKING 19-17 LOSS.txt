Eight seconds, Eagles Nation. That’s all that stood between Philadelphia and a massive win on the other side of the Atlantic. The kick was good. The sideline was celebrating. The fans who crossed an ocean were jumping in the stands. And then it all came crashing down. Everybody is talking about the kickoff. But there’s another decision hidden inside this loss that nobody is talking about enough. A quarterback decision. Nick Sirianni decided not to put the final seconds of that game in Jalen Hurts’ hands. And after the game, Hurts said five words that have all of Philadelphia buzzing. Quote, “Ultimately, that’s not my place.” So today on Second Floor, we’re digging into the QB decision that shaped the ending in London, what Hurts really meant, and the biggest question Sirianni has to answer this week.

Welcome in, everybody. This is James, and you’re on Second Floor. If you were just as frustrated watching that ending as I was, hit that like button and subscribe right now, because this conversation is only getting started and your voice needs to be part of it. Let’s get into it.

First, the quick recap, because we broke the kickoff down in detail in our last video. Sunday, Tottenham Hotspur Stadium, London. The Eagles came in at 2 and 2 after back-to-back losses to the Bears and the Rams. Not many people believed in them. And still, against the odds, with a patched-up offensive line and no Saquon Barkley or DeVonta Smith, they reached the final seconds with the lead. Jake Elliott hit a 48-yard field goal with eight seconds left. Eagles 17, Jaguars 16. Then the Eagles kept the kickoff in play, Ameer Abdullah returned it 53 yards to the Eagles’ 39, and Cam Little drilled a 57-yarder as time expired. Jaguars 19, Eagles 17.

Now, here’s where the quarterback part of this story comes in. Because the real question isn’t just why the Eagles kicked the ball the way they did. It’s why there were eight seconds left on the clock in the first place.

Let’s go back to the final drive. The Jaguars were using their timeouts to stop the clock. The Eagles were in field goal range. And the goal in that situation is simple. Burn every possible second, then kick the game-winner with as little time left as possible. Sirianni said it himself after the game. Ideally, you’d like to kick it with about three seconds left. But that’s not how it played out.

On one key play, Jalen Hurts hit rookie Makai Lemon, and Lemon was pushed out of bounds at the Jaguars’ 27-yard line. That stopped the clock. Former Eagles staffer Ryan Paganetti, who used to work on game management for this very team, called it out right away. He said the Eagles “CANNOT go out of bounds in that situation.” He said it was “still likely win, but that opened up a way to lose.” In his view, the field goal should have been the final play of the game.

And then there’s the other option. The quarterback option. Why didn’t the Eagles just let Hurts take the snap, hold the ball, maybe even take a knee or two, and drain the clock before the kick? That’s where Sirianni made his QB decision. A reporter asked him about it directly. And Sirianni explained that when you do that, there’s going to be push up the middle, and you’re probably going to lose two, three, four yards. It was already a long field goal, so pushing Elliott back even further wasn’t something they wanted. In his words, that wasn’t in their thought process at that particular time because of the risk of losing yards.

Instead, the Eagles ran the ball, hoping to pick up a first down so they could call a timeout with about five seconds left. The Jaguars stopped them. Elliott kicked with eight seconds on the clock. And you know the rest.

And to be fair to Makai Lemon, he’s a rookie. In most situations, getting out of bounds after a catch is exactly what you’re taught to do. It saves time, it gives your offense another play. But in that exact moment, with the Eagles already in field goal range and the Jaguars trying to stretch the clock, staying in bounds was worth more than the extra yards. That’s the kind of situational detail a veteran knows by instinct and a rookie learns the hard way. And honestly, that’s on the coaching too. Those situations should be drilled until they’re automatic. Sirianni admitted as much when he said there’s gray in these moments, quote, “We want in football to be as black and white as we possibly can, but there’s gray in it.”

So think about that, Eagles Nation. The head coach made a choice to keep the ball out of the quarterback’s hands at the end. No kneel-downs. No clock-draining. Run the ball, kick the field goal, and trust the kickoff coverage. Every one of those decisions went the wrong way.

And now, here’s the part that set Philadelphia on fire. After the game, Jalen Hurts was asked why the offense didn’t drain more clock on that last possession. And his answer was short, calm, and loaded with meaning. Quote, “Ultimately, that’s not my place.” He added, quote, “I’m honoring the play call, honoring the things that are coming in the game.” And then he said something that really made people stop. Quote, “When I’m up at night, I think about should I have said something?” And finally, quote, “This was one where it didn’t work in our favor.”

Read that again. “When I’m up at night, I think about should I have said something?” That’s a franchise quarterback, a Super Bowl MVP, telling the world he’s wondering whether he should have stepped in. That’s not a small thing. Quarterbacks at this level choose their words carefully, and Hurts more than most.

And the reaction was immediate. Some fans said Hurts is fed up with the coaching. Pro Football Network collected reactions from people calling it the start of a “mutiny on Nick.” Others defended Hurts for being honest. And some said it was a bad look for a quarterback to make it sound like the decision wasn’t his. Heavy even ran a headline saying Hurts threw his head coach under the bus.

Now, let me be clear about what Hurts actually said, because I think it’s important. He never named Sirianni. He never said the call was wrong. He said it wasn’t his place, and that he honors the play call. That’s what a disciplined quarterback is supposed to do. But the part about lying awake at night wondering if he should have spoken up? That tells you he cares. And maybe it tells you he wants a bigger voice in those moments.

Meanwhile, Sirianni took every bit of the blame. Quote, “I didn’t make the right decision and I lost that game.” He said he put it fully on himself. He said the guys fought their butts off, and that he let everyone down. He said it’ll stick with him for a long time. That’s the head coach protecting his quarterback, his kicker and his coverage team. And honestly, that’s exactly what he should do.

But here’s the big question. The huge QB decision Sirianni has to make this week isn’t about who starts. Jalen Hurts is the starter. Period. And he’s healthy enough to play, even after popping up on the injury report with a calf issue last week. The real decision is this. How much control does Sirianni give his franchise quarterback in the biggest moments?

Think about it. Hurts has been in this offense for years. He’s won a Super Bowl. He’s one of the smartest situational players in the league. If he’s lying awake at night thinking he should have said something, maybe the Eagles need a system where he’s empowered to say it. Where the quarterback and the head coach are on the same page on clock management before the drive even starts. Where, if Hurts sees a better way to finish a game, he has the freedom to speak up on the sideline.

And remember, this isn’t the first time a late-game decision has put the spotlight on Sirianni and his quarterback. Go back to Monday Night Football against the Falcons in September of 2024. The Eagles were up by three late, inside the red zone, with a chance to run out the clock. Instead, on third-and-3, Hurts threw a pass to Saquon Barkley that fell incomplete. The clock stopped. The Eagles kicked a field goal to go up six, the Falcons got the ball back with 1 minute and 39 seconds left, and Kirk Cousins marched 70 yards and found Drake London for the game-winning touchdown. Back then, Sirianni defended his decisions. Now, history repeats itself. Different setting, same bitter ending.

And let’s be honest about how Hurts played, because that’s part of this too. He finished 21 of 40 for 220 yards, a touchdown, and no interceptions. The touchdown was beautiful, a 62-yard catch-and-run to Darius Cooper. And after three turnovers in Chicago, a zero-turnover day matters. But he also missed an open Dontayvion Wicks in the second half, and the offense stalled after halftime for the third straight week. Pro Football Network ranks Hurts 26th among quarterbacks right now, with a 69.2 grade. That’s not the Jalen Hurts we’re used to.

But look at what he’s working with. A center making his NFL debut. An undrafted left guard making his first start. A rookie right tackle in his second start. No Saquon. No DeVonta Smith. No Hollywood Brown. His backup running back went down in the first half. That’s not an excuse. That’s context. And it’s why the second-half offense has to be a coaching fix as much as a quarterback fix.

Speaking of quarterbacks, remember that Sirianni already made a QB decision before London. On Friday, the Eagles released Andy Dalton. The team had been carrying four quarterbacks, and with all the injuries, they needed the roster spots. Dalton’s only pass for the Eagles came in Chicago, when Hurts was briefly evaluated for a concussion, and it was a goal-line interception. Now the quarterback room is clear. Hurts is the guy, Tanner McKee is the backup, and Cole Payton is the third option. If the Eagles ever need a backup this season, there’s no confusion about who it is.

Now let’s look at the wider criticism of Sirianni, because it’s been brutal. On the NFL on CBS, analyst Damien Harris said he couldn’t comprehend what he just watched. He called it, quote, “nothing short of coaching malpractice.” He also said, quote, “I don’t think he can be questioned enough about some of the decision-making.” Fans on social media went even further, with some saying Sirianni should be fired just for that clock management.

And there was another Sirianni decision that came back up after the final whistle. The travel plan. The Eagles flew to London late Friday night and arrived Saturday morning, spending only about 36 to 40 hours in England before kickoff. The Jaguars, on the other hand, left on Thursday and are staying in London for back-to-back games, because they play the Texans there next weekend. Sirianni said the plan was to get a full week of practice at home first, and that it gave his team, quote, “the best chance to win, taking everything into account, jet lag included.” After a loss like this, people are going to question that too. Personally, I don’t think the travel plan lost this game. The Eagles were winning with eight seconds left. But every decision gets examined when you’re losing.

And then there’s the bigger pattern. Three straight weeks without a second-half touchdown, according to the Inquirer’s Marcus Hayes. Against the Rams, a 20 to 10 lead turned into a loss with zero fourth-quarter points. In London, a 14 to 7 halftime lead slipped away. The Eagles hired Sean Mannion as offensive coordinator this year with the promise of a more modern, creative offense. So far, it hasn’t clicked. Meanwhile, Vic Fangio’s defense held the Jaguars to 19 points, with two strip-sacks from Jalen Carter, an interception from Quinyon Mitchell, and a huge goal-line stand right before halftime. The defense did enough to win. Again.

The damage in the standings is real. The Eagles are 2 and 3. That’s three straight losses, and the first time they’ve been under .500 since 2021. It was also Sirianni’s first loss to an AFC South team after starting 6 and 0 against that division. And it was a huge missed opportunity, because the Cowboys lost on Thursday night to Tampa Bay and dropped to 2 and 3 as well. The door was wide open to climb back into the NFC East race. Instead, the Eagles came home with more questions than answers.

Now, to be fair and transparent, let’s look at the other side.

First, the Eagles nearly won a game a lot of people expected them to lose. Jacksonville came in at 3 and 1, with one of the best defenses in the league. The Eagles were missing a huge chunk of their starters, and they still had the win in hand with eight seconds left. That says something about the coaching staff and about the culture.

Second, a 57-yard field goal is not an easy kick. Even with the return, Cam Little still had to drill it from very long range, and he’d missed an extra point just minutes earlier. Credit goes to the Jaguars too.

Third, Sirianni didn’t hide. He owned it completely. And Hurts, even with his comments, didn’t attack his coach. He said he honors the play call. This locker room isn’t broken. Players like Arnold Ebiketie even said the coverage team could have done a better job tackling the returner. When everybody is taking ownership, that’s a team that can still turn things around.

And fourth, there’s no sign the organization is thinking about making a change. Sirianni signed a contract extension after winning Super Bowl 59, and Jeffrey Lurie and Howie Roseman have stood by him through tougher moments than this. As loud as the outside noise is, his seat inside the building doesn’t seem to be shaking, at least not yet.

So here’s my take, and you can fight me in the comments.

I don’t think the answer is firing anybody. I think the answer is trust. Sirianni needs to trust his quarterback more in the biggest moments. Hurts has earned that. When a Super Bowl MVP is lying awake at night wondering whether he should have spoken up, that’s a sign the communication between the head coach and the quarterback in late-game situations needs to be better. Sit down this week. Go through every end-of-game scenario together. Kneel or no kneel. Out of bounds or stay in. Touchback or cover. Make sure Hurts knows the plan, believes in the plan, and feels free to speak up if he sees something different.

And think about how the best teams in this league handle it. The great quarterbacks don’t just run plays. They manage games. They know the clock, the timeouts, the field position, the kicker’s range, all of it, and the head coach trusts them to make the right call in the moment. Hurts is capable of being that guy. He’s shown it in Super Bowls. He showed it three weeks ago in Tennessee, when he drove the Eagles 65 yards in under two minutes with no timeouts and threw the game-winner with nine seconds left. Give that guy the keys at the end of games. Let him own those moments the way he owns the huddle.

That’s the QB decision that matters most right now. Not who’s under center, but how much voice the guy under center has.

And on the field, the fixes are clear. Get Hurts some help. Landon Dickerson is eligible to come back from injured reserve this week. DeVonta Smith could be back. Saquon Barkley’s hamstring isn’t a long-term injury. Give Hurts his weapons back, give him a stable line, and let him play free. And for goodness’ sake, find a second-half offense.

Next up, the Carolina Panthers. And this is now a game the Eagles simply have to win. Here’s what I’m watching. Number one, Hurts and Sirianni. Body language, communication, and how the late-game situations are handled. Number two, the second-half offense. Can Mannion finally find answers after halftime? Number three, who’s back? Dickerson, Smith and Barkley change everything. Number four, Makai Lemon. He made a big catch on that final drive, and he has to keep growing. And number five, the defense. Carter, Baun and Mitchell played like stars in London. If they keep that up, the Eagles will win plenty of games.

At the end of the day, the Eagles didn’t lose this game because of one kickoff. They lost because of an offense that vanishes in the second half, a week of chaos off the field, and a string of late-game decisions that put the head coach right in the middle of the fire. And at 2 and 3, the margin for error is gone.
